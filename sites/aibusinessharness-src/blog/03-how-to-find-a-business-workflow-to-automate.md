Most businesses start their AI journey in the wrong place. They pick the most impressive idea, like an AI receptionist, a fully automated sales process or a chatbot that "knows everything," and then wonder why it stalls.

The better first project is almost always smaller and more boring: a repetitive task with clear inputs, a clear finish line and a real cost when it slips.

## Step 1: List the work nobody enjoys

Spend a week jotting down every task that makes someone on your team sigh. Don't filter yet. Typical entries look like:

- Checking the inbox for new inquiries and forwarding them to the right person
- Following up on estimates that went quiet
- Copying details from a form into the CRM
- Chasing customers for missing information before a job
- Reminding people about appointments
- Compiling a weekly "where are we" summary from three different tools

Aim for fifteen to twenty items. The goal is volume.

## Step 2: Score each task on five questions

Give each task a score from 1 to 5 on these:

1. **Frequency.** How often does it happen? Daily beats monthly.
2. **Consistency.** Do most instances follow the same pattern? If every case is a judgment call, it's a poor first candidate.
3. **Cost of slippage.** What happens when it's late or forgotten? Lost revenue and unhappy customers score high.
4. **Data access.** Does the information live somewhere software can reach, like email, a CRM or a spreadsheet? Information that lives only in someone's head scores low.
5. **Risk if wrong.** Invert this one: a task where a mistake is easy to catch and fix scores high; one where a mistake could harm a customer or cost money scores low.

Add them up. Your top three are your shortlist.

## Step 3: Check the "finish line" test

For each shortlisted task, finish this sentence: *"This task is done when..."*

- "...every new inquiry is tagged and assigned within an hour." ✔ Clear.
- "...every open estimate older than five days has a follow-up drafted for review." ✔ Clear.
- "...customers are happier." ✘ Too vague to automate or measure.

If you can't write a clear finish line, the task needs process work before it needs AI.

## Step 4: Measure the baseline

Before touching any technology, write down how the task performs today. Keep it simple:

- How many times a week does it happen?
- How long does each instance take?
- How often does it get missed or delayed?
- What does a miss cost, roughly?

Two weeks of honest tallies beats any estimate. Without a baseline, you'll never know whether the automation helped.

## Step 5: Decide how much AI it really needs

Not every good candidate needs AI. Some are better served by a plain rule ("if estimate is older than five days, add to list"). AI earns its place when the task involves *reading and writing language*: understanding an inquiry, summarizing a thread, drafting a reply in your voice.

The strongest first workflows usually combine both: simple rules to find the work, AI to prepare it, and a person to approve it.

## Red flags for a first project

Skip these for now, even if they score well:

- **Anything that sends money or makes commitments** without review
- **Tasks that require access to everything** to work at all
- **Processes that change every month**
- **Work where you can't tell if the output is right** without redoing it yourself

## What a good first pick looks like

A typical winner: *"Every weekday morning, find estimates with no reply in five days, draft a short personalized check-in for each, and put them in a review queue for the office manager."*

It's frequent, consistent, costly when missed, easy to verify, and low-risk because a person approves every message. It's exactly the kind of workflow our 30-day pilot is built around.

## The takeaway

Start where the work is repetitive, the finish line is clear and a mistake is easy to catch. Prove value there, then expand. The flashy ideas will still be there, and they'll be far easier to build on top of a workflow that already works.
