Big AI transformation projects tend to stall. They try to change too much at once, they're hard to measure, and by the time anything ships, the team has lost interest.

A better approach is a tightly scoped pilot: **one workflow, one measurable problem, thirty days.** At the end, you have evidence, and a clear decision about whether to keep, change or stop.

Here's how to run one.

## Before you start: set the rules

Agree on three things up front:

1. **One workflow only.** Resist adding "while we're at it" items.
2. **A person approves anything customer-facing.** No exceptions during the pilot.
3. **A go/no-go review on day 30.** Decided in advance, so the pilot can't drift on indefinitely.

## Week 1: Discover and measure

**Goal:** choose the workflow and understand how it works today.

- List repetitive tasks and pick the strongest candidate: frequent, consistent, costly when missed, easy to verify. (Our guide on [finding the right workflow to automate](/blog/how-to-find-a-business-workflow-to-automate/) walks through a scoring method.)
- Write the finish line: "This task is done when..."
- Map the current steps, people and tools involved.
- **Record a baseline.** How often does it happen? How long does each take? How often is it late or missed?
- Identify which systems hold the data and what access the workflow would need.

**End of week check:** a one-page description of the workflow, its baseline numbers and its finish line.

## Week 2: Design and connect

**Goal:** build the workflow in a safe, controlled form.

- Design the steps: what triggers it, what it looks up, what it decides, what it prepares.
- Sort every action into tiers: runs automatically, needs approval, or off-limits.
- Set up the narrowest possible access: read-only where possible, drafts instead of sending.
- Write down your voice and rules: example replies, things to never say, when to escalate.
- Decide what gets logged and where exceptions go.

**End of week check:** the workflow runs end to end on sample data, with nothing connected to live customer communication.

## Week 3: Test with real, but controlled, work

**Goal:** run it on real inputs while a person reviews everything.

- Turn it on against live data with all customer-facing actions held for approval.
- Review every output. Approve, edit or reject, and note the reason for edits and rejections.
- Fix issues as they appear: missed cases, wrong classifications, tone problems.
- Watch the exception queue. What the workflow *can't* handle is as informative as what it can.

**End of week check:** a list of issues found and fixed, and early numbers on approval and edit rates.

## Week 4: Measure and decide

**Goal:** compare against the baseline and make a decision.

Gather the numbers:

- How many times did the workflow run?
- What share of outputs were approved with no edits? With light edits? Rejected?
- How much time did review take, compared with doing the task by hand?
- Did anything get missed that wouldn't have been before, or vice versa?
- What happened to the outcome you cared about: response time, follow-up coverage, errors?

Then make the call:

- **Keep and expand:** results are good, review is fast, the team trusts it. Plan the next step, such as more message types or a related workflow.
- **Keep and adjust:** promising, but specific problems need another round of fixes.
- **Stop:** the workflow didn't earn its place. That's a valid, valuable result, and it cost thirty days, not a year.

## What makes pilots fail

- **Scope creep.** Three half-built workflows are worth less than one finished one.
- **No baseline.** Without "before" numbers, every result is an opinion.
- **Too much access too soon.** Start narrow. Expand on evidence.
- **Nobody owns review.** Approvals that sit for days make the workflow look broken.
- **Judging on day three.** Early runs surface the fixable problems. Judge on the full month.

## After the pilot

A successful pilot isn't the finish line. It's the foundation. The workflow now has a track record, a log and a person who trusts it. From there, you can move to managed operation: ongoing monitoring, regular review of exceptions, and gradual expansion based on what the data shows.
